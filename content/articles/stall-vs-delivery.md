There are two ways most people in Singapore get durian: walk up to a stall and point at a fruit, or tap a few buttons and wait for a sealed box at the door. Both are normal. Both can get you excellent fruit or a disappointing box. The difference is where the risk sits and how much control you hand over.

The short version: a stall gives you control over *which fruit* you get. Delivery gives you control over *what you pay* and *when you eat*. Which one matters more depends on what you're buying for.

## What you actually get at a stall

At a stall you choose from the fruit in front of you. You can ask the seller to pick one, open it, look at the pods, and — at most stalls — taste before you commit. If the flesh looks tired or the colour is wrong, you can say no and try another one before any money moves.

That's the stall's big advantage: **you judge the actual fruit, not a description of it.** Nothing about Musang King, Black Thorn or D24 is standardised enough that a name alone tells you what's inside. Seeing the pods is the only real quality check there is, and our guide on [how to pick a good durian](/guides/how-to-choose-a-durian) covers what to look for.

The other advantage is freshness at the moment of eating. A durian opened in front of you and eaten at the stall's tables ten minutes later is as good as that fruit is ever going to be. Flesh starts to change once the husk is off — it dries, darkens at the edges, and loses some aroma — so the shorter the gap, the better.

What you give up is convenience and price certainty. Most stalls sell by whole-fruit weight, husk included, which means you don't know exactly what you'll pay until the fruit is chosen and weighed. That's fine when you know the rules, and it's where most of the classic [stall tricks](/guides/durian-scams-singapore) live when you don't.

## What you actually get with delivery

Most delivery services send deseeded or seeded pulp in sealed boxes, priced per box by flesh weight. Some also send whole fruit, and some offer frozen pulp. The fruit was chosen, opened and packed by someone else, usually hours before it reaches you.

The advantages are real:

- **Price is clear up front.** You're paying for flesh, by the box, at a number you saw before ordering. No husk weight, no scale to watch, no "per kg or per fruit?" conversation.
- **No travel.** For anyone without a stall nearby or without a car, this is the whole point. Durian is famously unwelcome on public transport, and a taxi home with an open box is a gamble on the driver's patience.
- **Easier for groups and gifts.** Ordering several boxes for a gathering is simpler than carrying them. Sealed containers also contain the smell far better than a plastic bag.
- **Some services offer a replacement policy** if the fruit is bad, which a stall you walked away from can't.

What you give up is the ability to reject a fruit before it's yours. You're trusting the packer's judgement on grade and freshness. And the fruit was opened earlier than it would have been at a stall, so even good delivery pulp is rarely at the same peak as a durian eaten at the table. That gap is small with a good service and noticeable with a careless one.

## Side by side

| | Stall | Delivery |
|---|---|---|
| Choose your own fruit | Yes — see and usually taste first | No — packed by the seller |
| Freshness at eating | Best possible if eaten on the spot | Opened hours earlier; depends on service |
| Price clarity | Depends on unit and weight; ask first | Per box, flesh only, known up front |
| Travel needed | Yes | No |
| Smell control | Poor, unless you eat there | Good, sealed containers |
| Recourse if the fruit is bad | At the table only, before you leave | Some services replace or refund |
| Group orders / gifts | Awkward to carry | Easy |
| Best for | Enthusiasts, premium varieties, eating on the spot | Convenience, groups, known suppliers |

## When a stall is the better call

**You're buying a premium variety for the first time.** If you're spending on Musang King, Black Thorn or Red Prawn, seeing the fruit before you pay is worth the trip. Variety substitution and grade inflation are much easier to spot in person than from a photo on a website. Our guide on [spotting fake Musang King](/guides/how-to-spot-fake-musang-king) is built around the in-person check.

**You want to eat it now.** Nothing a delivery box does beats a fruit opened at the table. If the point is the experience — a few people, a few varieties, a bin for the husks — go to the stall.

**You care about the specific lot.** Stall regulars get told when this week's batch is good and when to wait. That relationship doesn't exist with an app. The [directory](/directory) lists stalls by area so you can find the ones near you.

## When delivery is the better call

**You know the service.** Delivery quality varies far more by provider than by variety. Once you've found one whose boxes match what they promise, the downside mostly disappears.

**You're feeding a group at home.** Carrying six boxes back on a bus isn't realistic, and pulp in sealed containers is easier to serve than whole fruit that someone has to open.

**You want a predictable bill.** Per-box, flesh-only pricing makes it much easier to know what you're spending. It won't necessarily be cheaper — you're paying for the packing and the driver — but it will be what you expected.

**You're sending it to someone.** Gifting a whole fruit is theatre; gifting a sealed box is logistics. The box wins.

## Comparing prices fairly

This is where most people get it wrong. Stall prices are usually quoted per kilo of whole fruit. Delivery prices are usually per box of flesh. They aren't the same unit, and comparing the headline numbers directly will mislead you.

Most of a durian's weight is husk and seed, so a stall's per-kilo price covers far less edible fruit than the number suggests. How much less varies by variety and by individual fruit, which is exactly why a direct comparison is hard. The fair comparison is cost per gram of flesh you actually eat — and the only reliable way to get that from a stall purchase is to weigh what's left after opening.

For the going rate on each variety, check the [live price tracker](/) before ordering either way, and [trends](/trends) to see whether the market is climbing or easing this week. Stall prices move with each day's supply; a delivery menu is set in advance and may not move as quickly. When the market is flooded, that's one argument for walking to a stall.

## A sensible way to use both

Most regular eaters end up doing both:

1. **Use a stall to learn.** Taste varieties in person, figure out what you actually like, and find a seller you trust.
2. **Use delivery for repeats.** Once you know you want D24 or Musang King, and you've found a service that packs it properly, order the thing you already know you like.
3. **Go back to the stall for anything expensive or new.** A new variety, a premium grade, or a big occasion is worth the trip.

The mistake is treating either one as always better. A careless stall will sell you old fruit at full price, and a careless delivery service will send you pale, watery pulp in a nice box. The real choice isn't stall versus delivery — it's a seller you've tested versus one you haven't. The [sellers page](/sellers) is a good place to start building that list.
