Durian delivery in Singapore has gone from a WhatsApp number and a motorbike to a crowded market of websites, Instagram accounts, and app listings. Most of them show the same photos: glossy golden pods in a black tray, a Musang King logo, a "fresh from Pahang" line. The photos tell you nothing. The differences that matter are almost all invisible until the box arrives.

This guide is about what to check before you order, and what to check when the box lands — so your second order is with someone better than your first.

## The one thing that actually predicts quality

Everything below is useful, but if you only remember one filter, make it this: **how long between opening the fruit and it reaching your door?**

Durian flesh starts changing the moment the husk comes off. It dries at the edges, darkens, loses aroma, and the texture shifts. A pod opened at 4pm and eaten at 6pm is a different thing from the same pod opened at 9am. Nothing about packaging, branding, or price fixes that gap.

Good services open to order, or open in small batches on the day. Weaker ones open in bulk in the morning and work through the stock all day. You often can't tell from the site — but you can ask. A service that answers "we pack after your order comes in" and delivers in under two hours is telling you something real. One that dodges the question is also telling you something.

## Questions worth asking before you order

Most of these can be answered from the website in two minutes. If they can't, message them — how they reply is part of the answer.

**Is the price per box of flesh, or per whole fruit?** Almost all delivery is sold by flesh weight in a box, which is the honest unit. If a service quotes whole-fruit weight and delivers it opened, you're paying for husk you never see. Compare against stall prices carefully — see our guide on [stall vs delivery](/guides/stall-vs-delivery) for why the headline numbers aren't comparable.

**What grade, and do they say so?** Musang King is not one thing. If a listing just says "Musang King" with no grade, you're accepting whatever they packed. Services that state grade — and price different grades differently — are the ones being straight with you. Our [grades guide](/guides/msw-grades-explained) explains what A, B and C actually mean.

**What happens if the fruit is bad?** This is the single biggest advantage delivery has over a stall, and plenty of services don't offer it. A clear replace-or-refund policy, with a stated window (usually: report on the day, with photos), is worth more than a slightly lower price. No policy at all means all the risk is yours.

**Is it fresh or frozen?** Both are legitimate; they're just different products. Frozen pulp is consistent, available year-round, and fine for desserts. Fresh is better eaten as fruit. The problem is only when a service is vague about which one you're getting.

**Do they name the variety honestly?** "Premium grade" and "cat mountain king" aren't varieties. If the menu is full of house names you can't map to real cultivars, that's a flag. The [varieties guide](/guides/durian-varieties-singapore) covers what's actually grown and sold here.

## What good and weak services look like

| | Worth reordering | Worth skipping |
|---|---|---|
| Packing time | Opened after your order, or same-day in small batches | Opened in bulk that morning, sold all day |
| Pricing unit | Per box, flesh weight, stated clearly | Vague, or whole-fruit weight for opened fruit |
| Grades | Named and priced separately | One "Musang King" price, no grade |
| Bad-fruit policy | Stated, with a reporting window | None, or "case by case" |
| Variety names | Real cultivars | House names you can't verify |
| Delivery window | Narrow, and they hold to it | "Sometime today" |
| Photos | Their own, varying by batch | The same stock shot for a year |
| Season honesty | Tell you when supply is poor | Everything available, always |

That last row deserves emphasis. Singapore's supply follows Malaysian harvests, and there are stretches where good Musang King is genuinely scarce. A service that is happily selling every variety at every grade in a thin month is either sourcing something other than what it claims, or shipping fruit it shouldn't. Check where we are in the cycle on the [season guide](/guides/durian-season-singapore) before you order and you'll know when to be suspicious.

## Judging the box when it arrives

Open it immediately. Two reasons: the fruit is at its best right then, and any replacement policy almost certainly requires same-day reporting with photos.

What to look at:

- **Colour.** Even, deep colour for the variety, not pale and not browning at the edges. Edge browning means it was opened a while ago.
- **Surface.** Slightly glossy is right. Dry and slightly crusted means age. Sitting in liquid means the pods were bruised or over-ripe.
- **Smell.** Strong is correct. Sour, fermented, or alcoholic is not — that's fruit past its point.
- **Seed ratio.** Plenty of thin, flat seeds is what you're paying premium prices for. A box where most pods are mostly seed is poor selection, whatever the grade label says.
- **Consistency across pods.** A good packer sorts. If one pod is excellent and the next is pale and firm, they're filling boxes from mixed stock.

Texture is a matter of taste rather than quality — dry and dense versus soft and wet is a variety and ripeness thing, not a defect. Our guide on [dry vs wet durian](/guides/dry-vs-wet-durian) explains what you're actually tasting, so you don't report a good box as a bad one.

## Price: what you're actually paying for

A delivery box costs more than the equivalent flesh from a stall, and it should. You're paying for someone to select the fruit, open it, sort the pods, pack them, and drive across the island. That's real work and it's worth money.

What you should refuse to pay for is the same markup with none of the work — bulk-opened pulp in a nice tray, no grading, no policy.

Because prices move with supply, the useful habit is to check the [live price tracker](/) before ordering and [trends](/trends) to see which way the market is going this week. If wholesale is easing and a service hasn't moved its menu in a month, you're paying last month's price. If the market is spiking, a service still quoting its usual number is worth a second look at what's actually in the box.

## Ordering for a group

Most delivery gets bought for gatherings, so a few practical notes:

1. **Order by flesh weight, not fruit count.** Boxes are the right unit for planning. Our [portion guide](/guides/how-much-durian-per-person) has sensible amounts per person.
2. **Mix varieties across boxes rather than buying one big lot.** It costs about the same and it's a much better table — and it tells you what your group actually likes.
3. **Confirm the delivery window against when people arrive.** Pulp that sits on the counter for two hours before anyone eats it wastes the main thing you paid for.
4. **Have a plan for the containers.** Sealed boxes contain the smell well until they're opened and then not at all. Bag them and get them out of the flat the same night.

## Build a shortlist, not a favourite

The most useful thing you can do is order small from two or three services in the same month and compare. Same variety, same stated grade, same week. The differences are obvious side by side in a way they never are one box at a time.

Then stick with the winner for repeat orders and keep a second name for when the first is sold out. Supply is variable enough that any single service will have a bad week, and having a backup you've already tested beats gambling on a new one during a shortage.
