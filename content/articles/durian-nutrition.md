Durian has a reputation in Singapore that splits cleanly down the middle. One uncle says it's the king of fruits and full of goodness. Another says it's "heaty," fattening and basically dessert. Both are partly right, and neither is especially useful when you're standing at a stall deciding whether to get one fruit or two.

Here's what's actually in durian, how it compares to the fruit you'd normally eat, and what that means for a durian night.

## The short version

Durian is a fruit, but it doesn't eat like one. Most fruit is mostly water and a bit of sugar. Durian is denser: more carbohydrate, noticeably more fat than almost any other fruit, and a lot more calories per bite.

According to USDA FoodData Central, 100g of raw durian pulp comes in at roughly 150 kcal, with around 27g of carbohydrate, about 5g of fat, under 2g of protein and close to 4g of fibre. It's also a genuinely good source of potassium and carries useful amounts of vitamin C and several B vitamins.

So: nutritious, yes. Light, no.

## How it compares to other fruit

Numbers per 100g of edible flesh, rounded, from USDA data. These are reference values — any single fruit will vary.

| Fruit (100g) | Calories (approx.) | What stands out |
|---|---|---|
| Durian | ~150 kcal | High carbs, meaningful fat, high potassium |
| Avocado | ~160 kcal | Mostly fat, very little sugar |
| Banana | ~90 kcal | Carbs and potassium, almost no fat |
| Mango | ~60 kcal | Sugary but watery |
| Apple | ~50 kcal | Mostly water and fibre |

Durian sits in the same calorie bracket as avocado, but the make-up is different. Avocado's energy comes mostly from fat. Durian's comes mostly from carbohydrate — largely sugars and starch — with fat as a secondary contributor. That combination is exactly why it tastes like custard: sweet, rich and creamy at the same time.

## What about sugar?

This is the question most people actually mean when they ask about durian nutrition.

Most of durian's carbohydrate is sugar, and it's sweet in a way that registers immediately. That said, the reference databases don't all publish a clean sugar figure for durian, and the ones that do disagree. We're not going to invent a precise number.

What you can safely take away:

- **Durian is one of the sweeter fruits you can eat**, and it is carbohydrate-heavy per bite.
- **Sweetness varies a lot by variety.** A mild, sweet [D101](/guides/d101-durian-guide) and a bitter [XO](/guides/xo-durian-guide) are not going to land the same way, and a ripe, soft, "wet" fruit tends to taste sweeter than a firmer one. Our [dry vs wet guide](/guides/dry-vs-wet-durian) explains the texture side.
- **Bitterness doesn't mean less sugar.** Bittersweet varieties like Musang King and Black Thorn still carry plenty of sugar — the bitterness just balances it on the palate.

If you're managing blood sugar, treat durian as a sweet, carb-heavy food rather than a free-pass fruit, and ask your doctor or dietitian what fits your plan.

## The fat is not the villain

Durian's fat content surprises people, because fruit isn't supposed to have fat. At around 5g per 100g it's more than most fruits by a wide margin, though well below avocado or nuts.

It's not a reason to avoid durian. The fat is a big part of why the flesh feels so rich and why a small amount is satisfying. What matters is that it stacks on top of the carbohydrates rather than replacing them, which is why the calorie count climbs quickly.

## What's good about it

Durian isn't just empty sweetness. It brings real nutrients with it:

- **Potassium.** Durian is a strong source — comparable to or above banana per 100g.
- **Fibre.** Close to 4g per 100g, which is decent for a fruit.
- **Vitamin C.** A useful amount, though not in citrus territory.
- **B vitamins.** Durian is notably rich in thiamin (B1) compared to most fruit, with meaningful B6 as well.

It also contains sulphur compounds — the source of the smell, and the reason durian and alcohol don't mix well. Our [durian and alcohol guide](/guides/durian-and-alcohol) covers that in detail.

## The part nobody does the maths on: portions

A durian night rarely stops at 100g.

Think about how durian is actually eaten in Singapore: a table of friends, a couple of fruits, everyone reaching for "just one more seed." Each pod of flesh looks small, but the rich ones are dense, and it adds up fast. A few hundred grams of pulp is easy to put away in one sitting, and at roughly 150 kcal per 100g, that's a proper meal's worth of energy before anyone has thought about dinner.

Two things make this harder to judge:

1. **You buy by whole-fruit weight, not pulp.** Stall prices are quoted per kg of whole fruit, and a large share of that weight is husk and seed. A 2kg fruit gives you much less than 2kg of flesh — so you can't use the price tag to estimate how much you ate.
2. **Premium fruit encourages finishing.** When you've paid Musang King money, nobody wants to leave anything behind. That's a spending instinct, not an appetite.

## "Heatiness" and feeling unwell afterwards

In traditional Chinese medicine framing, durian is a "heaty" food, and plenty of people swear they feel warm, thirsty or sluggish after a big session. Whatever framework you use, the practical explanation is simple enough: you've just eaten a large amount of dense, sweet, fatty food in a hot climate.

The folk remedies — mangosteen as a "cooling" pairing, drinking water from the husk, coconut water — all have one sensible thing in common: they get you to slow down and drink fluids. That's good advice regardless of whether you believe in heatiness.

## Who should be more careful

Durian is fine for most healthy people in reasonable amounts. A few groups should think before a big session:

- **People managing diabetes or blood sugar** — it's a sweet, carb-dense food.
- **People on potassium-restricted diets**, such as some kidney patients — durian is high in potassium.
- **Anyone drinking alcohol with it** — see the alcohol guide above.
- **Anyone watching calories** — a casual session can quietly become the biggest meal of the day.

This is general information, not medical advice. If any of these apply to you, your doctor or dietitian is the right person to ask about specific amounts.

## The verdict

Durian is a genuinely nutritious fruit — rich in potassium, fibre, thiamin and vitamin C — that also happens to be one of the most calorie-dense and sweetest fruits on sale in Singapore. It isn't health food and it isn't junk food. It's a rich, energy-heavy treat that deserves to be eaten like one.

The practical move: decide how much you're going to eat before you get to the stall, share properly, and drink water. Then enjoy it. If you're still choosing where to go, the [directory](/directory) lists stalls by area.
