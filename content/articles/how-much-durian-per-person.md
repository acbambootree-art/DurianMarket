"How many kg ah?" is the first thing a seller asks, and most people answer with a number they picked on the walk over. Then one of two things happens: half the table is staring at empty husks twenty minutes in, or someone is driving home with three leftover fruits stinking up the car.

The problem is that you're buying one thing and eating another. Stalls weigh the **whole fruit** — husk, seeds and all. You eat the **flesh**, which is only a fraction of that weight. Once you plan around flesh instead of fruit, portioning gets easy.

## Whole fruit weight is mostly not food

A durian is a thick, heavy husk wrapped around a handful of seeds, with the flesh coating those seeds. The husk alone accounts for a big share of what goes on the scale, and the seeds are dense too.

As a rough working rule, expect somewhere around a quarter to a third of the whole-fruit weight to be edible flesh. It varies a lot: variety, fruit size, how many chambers are properly filled, and plain luck all move it. A fruit with a few empty or underdeveloped chambers can come in well under that. Treat the rule as a planning estimate, not a promise.

So when you buy 2 kg of whole fruit, you're really buying something like half a kilo of eating, give or take. That reframing is the whole guide in one sentence.

## A simple per-person rule of thumb

Appetite for durian is wildly uneven, so plan by the kind of eater rather than by headcount alone.

| Eater type | Whole fruit per person | Roughly how that feels |
|---|---|---|
| First-timer or "just a taste" | Around 0.3–0.5 kg | A few pods, curiosity satisfied |
| Casual eater | Around 0.5–1 kg | A proper serving, not stuffed |
| Enthusiast | Around 1–1.5 kg | A real session |
| Durian party regular | 1.5 kg and up | The person who asks for "one more" |

These are whole-fruit weights, since that's what the stall charges you for. They're rules of thumb built from how durian sessions tend to go, not measured data, so adjust for your crowd.

For a mixed group, add up the tiers rather than multiplying one number by headcount. Four enthusiasts and two first-timers is a very different order from six "average" people.

## It depends on the variety

Durian is rich, and some varieties are a lot richer than others. That changes how much people actually eat.

**Dense, bitter, intense fruit fills people up fast.** A dry, high-intensity Musang King or Black Thorn is satisfying in smaller amounts. People slow down after a few pods, even if they love it. If you're buying premium, you can often buy a bit less weight than you think.

**Sweeter, milder fruit goes down easier.** Softer, lighter varieties like [D101](/guides/d101-durian-guide) are easier to keep eating, and beginners will eat more of them. Budget varieties like [D13](/guides/d13-durian-guide) are also where it's easiest to over-order, because the lower price makes an extra fruit feel harmless.

Texture matters too. Dry, sticky flesh is more concentrated than waterlogged flesh, so a dry week satisfies with less. Our [dry vs wet durian guide](/guides/dry-vs-wet-durian) explains how to tell which you're getting.

## Mix varieties instead of going big on one

The most useful portioning trick isn't about kg at all. Instead of one large order of a single premium variety, split the same budget across two or three.

This works for a few reasons:

- **People eat more when there's contrast.** Palate fatigue on one intense variety is real. Switching between bitter and sweet keeps a session going.
- **Beginners have a safe option.** Not everyone at the table wants the strongest fruit, and a milder variety means nobody is stuck.
- **You waste less.** When one variety turns out below par that week, you haven't bet the whole order on it.

A common approach for a group: one premium fruit for the enthusiasts, plus a cheaper, milder variety to fill out the volume. Check the [live price tracker](/) before you decide the split, since the gap between varieties moves week to week. ## Ordering pulp by the box

Delivery services and some stalls sell de-husked flesh in boxes. This is the one situation where the weight you pay for is roughly the weight you eat.

That makes it tempting to compare box prices to whole-fruit prices per kg, but they aren't the same unit. A per-kg whole-fruit price looks much cheaper because most of that weight is husk and seed. To compare fairly, estimate the flesh you'd get from the whole fruit first.

Boxed pulp also takes the chamber lottery off your plate. What you give up is the ability to inspect the fruit before it's opened. Our [stall vs delivery comparison](/guides/stall-vs-delivery) covers that trade-off in more detail.

## Leftovers are not a disaster, but plan for them anyway

Over-ordering slightly is better than under-ordering. Nobody remembers the three extra pods; everybody remembers running out.

That said, durian doesn't hold well at room temperature once opened. Flesh you won't finish should come off the husk and go into an airtight container in the fridge soon after the session. Wrap it well, because durian will happily share its smell with everything else in there. Eat it within a day or two for the best texture.

If you routinely end up with leftovers, that's a signal to buy one tier lower next time, not to force it down.

## A few sanity checks before you say a number

**Think in fruits, not just kg.** Ask the seller roughly how many fruits your weight works out to. A couple of large fruits and several small ones give very different eating experiences, and more fruits means more chances that one is excellent.

**Account for what else is on the table.** A durian-only session after dinner needs less than a durian party that is dinner.

**Go easy on first-timers.** Durian is rich and filling. Someone new to it will usually tap out after a few pods, and buying heavily "just in case they like it" is how leftovers happen.

**Start smaller and top up.** At a stall, this is the lowest-risk move. Open a modest order, see how the table is doing, then ask for another fruit. Sellers are used to it, and you get to pick the second fruit knowing what the first one tasted like.

## The short version

Buy for flesh, not fruit weight. Assume only a fraction of what the scale says ends up in your mouth. Plan by type of eater, lean lighter on rich premium varieties, and split your order across two varieties if you're feeding a group.
