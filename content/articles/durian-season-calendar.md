Every year someone asks for "the durian calendar" as if there were a printed one pinned up in Geylang. There isn't. The fruit follows the weather in Malaysian orchards, and the weather does not check dates.

But a rough calendar is still useful. The broad shape of the year repeats: a big mid-year harvest, a smaller one near the year end, and lean stretches in between. If you know that shape, you can decide when to splurge, when to buy casually, and when to leave it alone.

This guide lays the year out month by month. Treat every month as "usually," not "always." For the reasons the dates drift, see our [guide to durian season in Singapore](/guides/durian-season-singapore).

## Where Singapore's durian comes from

Nearly all the fresh durian sold here crosses the Causeway from Peninsular Malaysia, mostly Johor and Pahang, with fruit from other states arriving at their own points in the year. Singapore's prices are therefore set by Malaysian harvests, plus the usual daily swings of a perishable market.

That is why a calendar of *Malaysian* harvests is really a calendar of *Singapore* prices. When the orchards drop heavily, stalls here get more fruit than they can comfortably sell, and rates soften. When the orchards go quiet, the few good lots that arrive get priced accordingly.

## The year at a glance

| Months (approx.) | Harvest phase | Typical SG prices | Buying approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan – Feb | Tail of year-end season, then lean | Firming up | Buy early in the window or wait |
| Mar – Apr | Off-season | Highest of the year | Buy only if you must; consider frozen |
| May | Main season building | High but starting to move | Watch direction, don't commit yet |
| Jun – Aug | Main season peak | Lowest of the year | Splurge window — big buys, premium varieties |
| Sep – Oct | Main season tapering | Rising again | Casual buying, fewer varieties |
| Nov – Dec | Smaller year-end season (unreliable) | Mixed | Bonus if it arrives; don't plan around it |

## Month by month

### January and February

Some years the smaller year-end harvest runs into January, and there is decent fruit around at reasonable rates. Other years it has already faded. Chinese New Year falls in this window, and demand for gifting fruit and hampers tends to rise around it, which does not help prices.

By February, you are usually heading into the lean stretch. If you see good fruit at a rate you like in January, that is often a better deal than anything you will see for the next three months.

### March and April

This is the thinnest part of the year for most buyers. Fewer stalls are properly stocked, variety choice narrows, and prices sit high. Durian is still available — delivery services and a handful of stalls keep going — but you pay for the privilege.

This is also when misrepresented fruit is most tempting for a seller. Scarce premium stock means more incentive to call something Musang King when it isn't. Our [guide to spotting fake Musang King](/guides/how-to-spot-fake-musang-king) earns its keep in these months.

If you just want durian and don't need a specific variety, frozen pulp from the previous season is an honest option here. See [can you freeze durian](/guides/can-you-freeze-durian) for how well it holds up.

### May

The first wave of the main season often starts arriving. Supply looks like it has turned, and the first price drops appear. Hold off on the big purchase.

The earliest fruit off the trees is often uneven in quality, and prices have only started to fall. A couple of weeks' patience usually buys you both better fruit and a better rate. Watch the direction on [trends](/trends) rather than the level.

### June, July and August

This is durian season as most Singaporeans mean it. Volume is heavy, the full range of varieties is on the table at the same time, and stalls compete on price because they have fruit to clear every night.

This is the window for:

- **The expensive varieties.** If Musang King, Black Thorn or Red Prawn is your annual treat, this is when premium fruit is most plentiful and most fairly priced. Our [Musang King price guide](/guides/musang-king-price-guide) explains how to judge whether a per-kg rate is fair.
- **Group buys and durian parties.** Bulk buying makes the most sense when supply is deep. Use the [portion guide](/guides/how-much-durian-per-person) to size it.
- **Trying something new.** Peak season is the cheapest time to experiment with a variety you've never had. The [variety comparison](/guides/durian-varieties-singapore) covers what's usually in circulation.

Even inside the peak, prices move daily. A heavy drop or a rainy weekend can shift the rate by a noticeable amount within a few days. The [guide to why durian prices change](/guides/why-durian-prices-change) covers what drives those short swings.

### September and October

The main harvest is tapering. Fruit is still around, but the table gets narrower week by week and prices climb back up. Late-season lots can be good, but you'll also see more clearance fruit that has been sitting a day too long.

Buy for eating now rather than for the freezer. And check the opened fruit before you pay — the checks in [how to pick a good durian](/guides/how-to-choose-a-durian) matter more when the stock is older.

### November and December

Many years bring a smaller second harvest around the year end. When it comes, it can be a pleasant surprise: decent fruit at better rates than the lean months. When it doesn't, November and December feel much like the off-season.

Don't plan a big event around this window. If the fruit shows up and the trend is falling, enjoy it. If not, wait.

## How to actually use the calendar

A calendar tells you roughly when to pay attention. The market tells you when to buy. Combine the two:

1. **Use the calendar for planning.** Put your big durian spend — the premium varieties, the party — in the middle of the year.
2. **Use trends for timing.** Once the season is building, check [trends](/trends). A rate that has fallen for two weeks straight is a better signal than any month on a chart.
3. **Use the tracker on the day.** Before you leave the house, check today's rate on the [live tracker](/) so you know what fair looks like when you arrive.
4. **Compare areas when supply is tight.** In lean months, the spread between different parts of Singapore widens. The [area directory](/directory) shows where sellers are.

## Why the calendar is never exact

Three things shift the dates every year:

- **Weather during flowering.** Heavy rain at the wrong time thins the crop months later. A dry spell before flowering often leads to a heavier one.
- **Regional staggering.** Different growing areas peak at different times, so the season in Singapore is several overlapping waves rather than one.
- **Tree cycles.** Durian trees don't produce evenly year to year. A bumper year is often followed by a lighter one.

So if a seller tells you in March exactly which week the season will peak, they are guessing. The shape of the year is reliable. The exact weeks are not.

## The short version

Big season mid-year, a smaller and less reliable one near the year end, and expensive lean months around March and April. Save your big spending for June to August. Don't buy the first fruit of the season. Watch price direction on [trends](/trends), and check the [live tracker](/) before every trip. The calendar tells you when to start looking. The prices tell you when to buy.
