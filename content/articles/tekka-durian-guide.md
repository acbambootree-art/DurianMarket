Tekka is the durian that stall regulars order without looking at the board. It doesn't get a display case, it doesn't get a queue, and it almost never gets a hype premium — but ask an uncle who has been cutting durian for twenty years what he eats on a slow Tuesday, and Tekka comes up often enough to be a pattern.

It's also one of the few durians in Singapore sold almost entirely by name rather than by code. Here's what **Tekka** actually is, and how not to buy a bad one.

## What is Tekka?

Tekka is a Malaysian variety, sold in Singapore under the Hokkien name and its English translation, **Green Bamboo**. *Tek kha* literally means "bamboo foot" — the same phrase behind Tekka Market in Little India, which was named for bamboo that once grew there. The durian and the market share a phrase, not a history; nobody is growing Tekka in Serangoon Road.

Unlike D24, D13 or D197, you will rarely see Tekka written as a number on a stall sign. It's a named variety in the trade, and that's how it's priced and sold. On most boards it sits in the mid band — above the budget orange varieties, below the premium tier. Check the [live price tracker](/) for where it's landing this week.

## What it tastes like

Tekka is a soft, pale, sweet-leaning durian with a bitter edge that shows up late rather than up front:

- **Flavour:** sweet first, with a mild bitterness on the finish. Not the aggressive bitter of the premium varieties — more of a savoury undertone that keeps it from tasting like dessert.
- **Colour:** pale to light yellow. Noticeably paler than the golden flesh of Musang King and nowhere near the orange of D101. If it comes out deep orange, it isn't Tekka.
- **Texture:** soft, wet and creamy. It gives easily and coats the mouth rather than sitting firm on the tongue.
- **Aroma:** moderate to strong. Present in the room, but it doesn't carry the pungency that clears a HDB corridor.
- **Seeds:** often small, which is where a lot of the value sits — more flesh per kilogram than the seed-to-flesh ratio you get from a variety with the same price tag.

That combination — creamy, mildly bitter, small-seeded, mid-priced — is the entire argument for Tekka. It gives you a hint of the complexity that people pay premium money for, at a price that doesn't require a decision.

## Tekka vs the varieties you already know

| | Tekka | D101 | Red Prawn (D175) | Musang King (D197) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flesh colour | Pale yellow | Bright orange | Orange-pink | Golden yellow |
| Flavour | Sweet, mild bitter finish | Sweet, no bitterness | Sweet, mild bitterness | Intense bittersweet |
| Texture | Soft, wet, creamy | Soft, creamy | Creamy, softer | Dense, fudgy |
| Aroma | Moderate–strong | Moderate | Moderate | Strong |
| Seeds | Often small | Inconsistent | Mixed | Mostly small |
| Price tier | Mid | Budget–mid | Mid | Premium |

The closest neighbour on a stall table is [Red Prawn](/guides/red-prawn-durian-guide) — same tier, similar creaminess, similar mild bitterness. The split is colour and character: Red Prawn is orange-pink and sweeter, Tekka is pale and drier in flavour despite being wetter in texture. Buyers who find Red Prawn a touch too sweet usually end up on Tekka.

If you're coming up from the budget tier, [D101](/guides/d101-durian-guide) is the honest comparison. D101 is purely sweet with no bitter finish at all. Tekka is where the bitterness starts — a small step, not the leap that [Musang King](/guides/musang-king-vs-d24) asks you to take.

## Why it stays cheap relative to how it tastes

Tekka has no marketing behind it. There's no origin story, no "old tree" claim, no colour so distinctive that it photographs well. The name is a Hokkien phrase most people under forty don't parse, and the English translation sounds like a plant, not a fruit.

That's the whole reason it's affordable. Nothing about the fruit is second-rate — it simply lost the branding contest. Compare that with the pricing logic behind [Black Thorn and Musang King](/guides/black-thorn-vs-musang-king), where a large share of what you're paying for is name recognition and constrained supply.

The practical consequence: Tekka is one of the better value-per-kilogram bets on most Singapore stall boards, and it moves less violently on price than the premium tier does. Watch that gap on the [trends page](/trends) — in peak-demand weeks, the mid tier holds while the top spikes.

## How to pick a good one

Tekka is soft and wet, which means it has a shorter window between ripe and gone than the firm varieties do.

- **Ask for it opened before you pay.** Every time. Pale yellow flesh confirms the variety on the spot — that's the fastest check you have.
- **Creamy is right, watery is not.** The flesh should be soft but still hold its shape when lifted. Pooling or slumping means it's past it.
- **Smell for sourness.** Tekka's aroma is strong enough to mask a lot, so pay attention. A fermented or alcoholic edge is spoilage, not character.
- **Check the seeds.** Small seeds are a Tekka selling point, but it varies by fruit. Look at what you're actually being handed, not what the variety is known for.
- **Check the flesh edges.** Browning or a skin forming means the fruit has been open too long or sitting out in the heat.

Fakery isn't the risk here — nobody counterfeits a variety that doesn't command a premium. The risk runs the other way: pale-fleshed, unlabelled fruit sold at premium prices under a vaguer name. Pale yellow, soft, mildly bitter flesh is mid-tier durian whatever the card says. Compare what stalls near you are asking in the [area directory](/directory) before you accept a number.

## When to buy it

Tekka follows the main Malaysian harvests like everything else. Because it's soft and wet, it travels and waits less gracefully than the firm varieties — buy it in the thick of season from a stall that's turning over stock, not in a thin week from a table that's been picked at all afternoon.

Its best moment is the peak-demand stretch, when the premium tier prices climb on hype and the mid tier doesn't. That's when the value gap opens widest, and it's the argument for walking past the front row.

## Who should buy Tekka

**Buy it if:**

- You want a hint of bitterness without paying the premium tier for it
- You've outgrown the purely sweet budget varieties
- You like creamy, wet texture over dense and fudgy
- You care more about value per kilogram than about the name on the sign
- You're buying for yourself rather than to impress a table

**Skip it if:**

- Bitterness is the whole point of durian for you — go straight to the premium end
- You want firm, chewy flesh
- You're buying off-season from a tired-looking display
- Somebody at the table needs a durian with no bitter finish at all

## The bottom line

Tekka is a pale, creamy, mildly bitter mid-tier durian that costs less than it tastes, because it never got a marketing department. It's the sensible upgrade from the sweet budget varieties and the sensible alternative when the premium tier spikes. Buy it opened, buy it in season, and buy it from a stall that's moving fruit.
