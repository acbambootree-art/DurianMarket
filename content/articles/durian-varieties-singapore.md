Walk up to any durian stall in Singapore and you're looking at a whiteboard with eight to fifteen names on it, half of them numbers, some of them in Hokkien, and none of them explained. The uncle will tell you what's good today. That's usually honest advice, but it's also advice shaped by what he needs to move.

This is the map. Every variety you're realistically going to see on a Singapore board, what it actually tastes like, and where it sits on price — so you can walk up already knowing which two or three names you're choosing between.

## How the board is organised (even when it doesn't look like it)

Stall boards look chaotic because they mix three naming systems. There are the D-numbers from the Malaysian registration system (D24, D13, D101, D197), the trade nicknames (Musang King, Red Prawn, Green Bamboo), and the marketing names that stalls invented (Black Gold, XO). Some varieties have all three.

Underneath that, there are really only three price bands:

- **Premium tier** — Musang King, Black Thorn, Black Gold. The names people queue for.
- **Upper-mid** — D24, XO, Red Prawn, Golden Phoenix. Serious durian, without the premium markup.
- **Everyday** — D13, D101, Tekka, and whatever the stall has in volume that week.

The bands move against each other. When Musang King spikes, the lower bands often barely budge — which is exactly when the mid-tier becomes the smart buy. That relationship is visible on the [trends page](/trends), and it's worth a look before you commit.

## The premium tier

**Musang King (D197)** is the default answer and mostly deserves it: golden-yellow flesh, dense and fudgy, intensely bittersweet, small seeds in the good fruit. It's the variety everything else gets compared to, which is also why it's the most counterfeited name on any board. If you only ever learn one durian, learn this one — and learn what real ones look like before you pay premium money for a fake.

**Black Thorn (Ochee, D200)** is the challenger. Creamier and smoother than Musang King, often sweeter, with a longer finish and a distinctive small dark protrusion at the base of the fruit. Fans argue it's the better durian; it's certainly the more delicate one. Our [head-to-head comparison](/guides/black-thorn-vs-musang-king) goes through the differences properly.

**Black Gold** is Musang King — specifically, older-tree Musang King selected for a darker, denser, more intensely bitter profile. Whether the premium over standard Musang King is worth it depends entirely on whether the stall is actually grading honestly, which is the whole problem. The [Black Gold guide](/guides/black-gold-durian-guide) covers what to check.

## The upper-mid tier

**D24** is the balanced one — bittersweet in proportion, creamy but firmer than the wet varieties, pale yellow flesh. It's the safe order for a mixed table and the reference point most experienced buyers use. If you want the direct comparison against the premium tier, we did it in [Musang King vs D24](/guides/musang-king-vs-d24).

**XO** takes D24's lineage and pushes it much further: pale, wet, strongly bitter, with a fermented alcoholic finish that gives it the cognac name. People love it or refuse it. See the [XO guide](/guides/xo-durian-guide) for how to tell "boozy on purpose" from "spoiled".

**Red Prawn (Ang Heh)** is the orange-fleshed one — sweet, creamy, mild bitterness, a slightly sticky texture. It's the crowd-pleaser of the upper-mid band and one of the easiest varieties to identify on sight. Full notes in the [Red Prawn guide](/guides/red-prawn-durian-guide).

**Golden Phoenix (Jin Feng, D198)** is bitter without the alcohol note, with pale flesh, a wet texture and a strong aroma out of proportion to its size. Cheaper than the premium tier and genuinely bitter, which makes it the value pick for bitterness chasers. More in the [Golden Phoenix guide](/guides/golden-phoenix-durian-guide).

## The everyday tier

**D13** is the orange-fleshed budget favourite — sweet, firm, low bitterness, small seeds more often than its price suggests. It's the variety that makes a large order affordable. The [D13 guide](/guides/d13-durian-guide) has the detail.

**D101** is the beginner's durian: sweet, mild, creamy, minimal bitterness, orange-ish flesh. If you're feeding someone who has never eaten durian and you want them to come back, this is the one. See the [D101 guide](/guides/d101-durian-guide).

**Tekka (Green Bamboo)** is soft, wet and creamy, sweet up front with a mild bitter finish — more complexity than its price band usually buys. Covered in the [Tekka guide](/guides/tekka-durian-guide).

You'll also see **Kampung** or **kampong** durian, which isn't one variety at all — it's the catch-all for unregistered village-grown fruit. Quality is genuinely unpredictable. Some of the best durian anyone eats is kampung; so is some of the worst. It's a lottery ticket, priced like one.

Thai **Monthong** shows up mainly in frozen and dessert form rather than on stall boards. Mild, sweet, firm, low aroma — closer to a fruit than to what Singaporeans mean by durian.

## How to actually choose

Skip the names for a second and answer two questions.

**How much bitterness do you want?** That single axis does most of the work. Sweet end: D101, D13, Red Prawn. Middle: Tekka, D24, Black Thorn. Bitter end: Golden Phoenix, Musang King, XO, Black Gold.

**How much are you willing to lose if it's a bad fruit?** This is the question people skip. A disappointing everyday-tier durian costs you a small annoyance. A disappointing premium-tier one costs real money — and the premium tier is where the fakes and the mislabelling live. If you're buying at a stall you don't know, buying mid-tier is not settling; it's sensible.

The practical move for a table of four or more: one premium fruit to enjoy, two mid or everyday fruits for volume. You get the experience without paying premium rates for the fifth kilo, when nobody's palate is paying close attention anyway.

## Before you buy

Always have the fruit opened before you pay, whatever the variety. Check the flesh colour matches what you're being told you bought. And check what stalls are actually asking this week — the gaps between tiers move constantly, and a variety that's poor value in a hype week can be the obvious pick a fortnight later.
