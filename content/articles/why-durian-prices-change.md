Most things you buy in Singapore have a price that holds for weeks. Durian does not. A stall can quote you one rate on Tuesday evening and a noticeably different one on Saturday afternoon, with no dishonesty involved at either visit. Buyers who don't expect this assume they're being played. Usually they aren't — they're just shopping in a market that reprices daily.

Durian is priced the way fresh seafood is priced: perishable stock, unpredictable supply, and a seller who has to clear the table before the fruit turns. Once you see the mechanics, the swings stop looking random and start looking usable.

## Durian is a perishable with no shelf life to speak of

This is the fact everything else hangs off. A durian that dropped from the tree is on a clock measured in days, not weeks. Flesh softens, the flavour flattens, and what was premium stock on Thursday is clearance stock by Saturday.

A stall holding fruit is therefore holding a depreciating asset. It cannot wait out a slow day the way a shop with tinned goods can. That single constraint explains most of what you'll see: aggressive evening discounting, reluctance to over-order, and prices that respond to today's crowd rather than this month's average.

## The supply side changes overnight

Singapore's durian comes across the Causeway. That supply chain is short, which is good for freshness and terrible for price stability.

**How much fruit dropped.** Durian isn't picked to a schedule — the ripe fruit falls, and it falls in waves. A heavy drop in the source orchards means a lot of fruit arriving at once, and prices soften within a day or two. A thin drop tightens supply just as quickly. This is the biggest single driver, and it happens on a timescale of days.

**What the weather did.** Rain during the drop damages fruit on the ground and reduces the proportion worth selling, which cuts the volume of *good* lots even when total volume looks fine. Wet weather in Singapore also thins the evening crowd, which hits demand at the same time.

**Transport and the checkpoint.** Fruit that arrives late arrives with less of the evening left to sell it in. Delays don't change the total supply much, but they change what's on the table at 7pm, which is when most of the buying happens.

**Which region is producing.** Different Malaysian growing areas peak at different points, so the mix of what's available shifts through the year. Our [durian season guide](/guides/durian-season-singapore) covers that longer cycle; the daily swings sit on top of it.

## The demand side changes on a weekly rhythm

Supply is unpredictable. Demand is almost boringly regular, which is why it's the easier half to plan around.

Weekend evenings are the peak. Friday and Saturday nights bring the crowds, and stalls know it — they order more and have less reason to discount. Weekday afternoons are the trough, and public holidays behave like extended Saturdays.

Late evening is its own effect regardless of the day. Fruit that hasn't sold by closing is worth much less tomorrow, so the last hour is where the real flexibility lives. That's clearance, not charity: you're buying fruit with less time left on it. If you're eating it tonight, that's a genuinely good trade.

## Grade is a price, not a label

A lot of apparent price movement isn't movement at all — it's a different grade being quoted.

"Musang King" is a variety, not a quality tier. Within any variety, stalls sort by pod fullness, flesh condition, fruit size and freshness, and the spread between a stall's best stock and its ordinary stock can be wide. Grading isn't standardised across Singapore, so one stall's premium is another's regular.

This is where buyers most often think they've found a bargain or been overcharged, when in fact they were quoted two different products. Before comparing two prices, establish that you're comparing the same grade, the same freshness, and the same quoting style — per-kg whole fruit, per-kg deshelled, or flat per fruit. Our [Musang King price guide](/guides/musang-king-price-guide) breaks down those quoting styles, because mixing them up is the fastest way to draw the wrong conclusion.

## What each factor actually does

| Size of the drop in Malaysia | Days | No — but you can check [trends](/trends) before you go |
| Rain during harvest | Days | No |
| Day of the week | Weekly, predictable | Yes — weekdays are cheaper |
| Hour of the evening | Same day | Yes — late is cheaper, and older |

## Why two stalls in different areas quote differently

Location is the one price difference that isn't about the fruit.

Rent differs across Singapore, and so do the crowds. A stall in a high-footfall, high-rent spot needs a higher margin per fruit than one on a quieter street, even when both bought from the same supplier that morning. Tourist-facing locations price accordingly. Areas with several competing stalls in a row tend to price tighter, because walking twenty metres is a real option for the buyer.

None of that is a scam — it's just where you chose to shop. The [area directory](/directory) is the fastest way to see what different parts of the island are asking on the same day, which is the only fair comparison.

## How to actually use the volatility

Four habits, none of which require haggling:

**Check before you go, not when you're standing there.** The [live tracker](/) tells you where the rate is landing now, and [trends](/trends) tells you which way it's heading. A falling market is a good week to wait a few days. A rising one is a reason to buy tonight.

**Buy on a weekday if you can.** Same fruit, lighter crowd, more attention from the seller, and a stall with more reason to make a sale.

**Ask what arrived today.** This is the single most useful question at a durian stall. It sorts fresh stock from held stock, and it tells you whether the price you're being quoted reflects fruit at its best or fruit on its way out.

**Judge by cost per kilo of flesh, not by the sign.** A cheap whole fruit with a thick shell and fat seeds can cost more per bite than an expensive deshelled portion. Do this arithmetic once or twice and you'll stop being impressed by low headline numbers.

## When a price change *is* a red flag

Daily movement is normal. A few things still deserve suspicion.

A rate far below everything else in the area usually means something specific: a cheaper variety being sold under a premium name, bottom-of-the-crop fruit, old stock, or previously-frozen pulp quoted as fresh. Any of those can be a fair deal if disclosed. The problem is a real discount with no disclosure — which is why it's worth knowing [how to spot fake Musang King](/guides/how-to-spot-fake-musang-king) before you're tempted by a number that seems too good.

The other flag is a price that changes *after* the fruit is opened. Confirm the rate and the weight before anything is cut. Once it's open, you've lost the ability to walk away, and a stall that reprices at that moment is not making an honest mistake.

## The short version

Durian prices move daily because durian is perishable, its supply arrives in unpredictable waves, and demand spikes on a weekly rhythm the sellers can read as well as you can. You can't control the harvest, but you can control when you shop, which area you shop in, whether you're comparing like with like, and whether you confirm the number before the fruit is cut. Check [the tracker](/) and [trends](/trends) before you leave, and the volatility works for you instead of against you.
