Every Musang King stall in Singapore seems to have its own ladder. Grade A, Grade B, Grade C. Premium and Regular. "Best," "Special," "Top." Sometimes two or three of these on the same signboard, each with its own price.

Here's the part that matters before anything else: **there is no official Musang King grading system.** No agency in Singapore or Malaysia defines what Grade A means, inspects fruit against it, or stops anyone from using the words. Every grade you see is the stall's own opinion about its own stock.

That doesn't make grades useless. It makes them a sorting system you need to understand, not a guarantee you can rely on.

## What a grade is really measuring

Musang King (Mao Shan Wang, D197) is a single variety. Grades don't separate different fruit — they separate better and worse examples of the same fruit. When a seller sorts a lot, they are generally looking at the same handful of traits.

**Pod fullness.** How many chambers have well-formed pods, and how plump those pods are. A fruit with every chamber full ranks higher than one with gaps or a couple of shrivelled pods.

**Flesh colour.** Deep, even golden-yellow is what buyers expect from good Musang King. Paler or patchy flesh gets sorted down.

**Texture.** Dry, dense, slightly sticky flesh is prized. Wetter, watery flesh usually lands lower. Our [dry vs wet durian guide](/guides/dry-vs-wet-durian) explains what those textures signal.

**Seed size.** Small, flat seeds mean more flesh for the weight you pay for. Fat seeds pull a fruit down.

**Condition.** Browning, dried skin on the pod surface, bruising, bugs, or fruit that has sat too long. These are the fastest ways to fall out of the top grade.

**Size and shape.** Some stalls factor in fruit size and whether the husk is well-shaped, since a lopsided fruit often means uneven pods inside.

Bitterness and intensity come into it too, but that part is taste-based and inconsistent — one seller's "strong" is another's "normal."

## Grade A vs B vs C at a glance

Because nothing is standardised, treat this table as what the labels *usually* point to, not what they promise.

| | Grade A / Premium | Grade B / Regular | Grade C / Budget |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pods | Most or all chambers full, plump | Some gaps or thinner pods | Noticeable gaps, uneven pods |
| Flesh colour | Deep, even gold | Gold, may be uneven | Paler or patchy |
| Texture | Dry, dense, sticky | Creamy, can be softer | Often wetter or less intense |
| Seeds | Small, flat | Mixed | Can be larger |
| Surface condition | Clean, fresh-looking | Minor marks | May show age or damage |
| Price | Highest at the stall | Middle | Lowest |
| Best for | Special occasions, experienced eaters | Most buyers, most days | Big groups, cooking, desserts |
| Biggest risk | Paying top price for ordinary fruit | Getting a C quoted as a B | Getting fruit that's simply old |

Not every stall uses three tiers. Many just split into "premium" and "regular." Some have a fourth tier above A, and names like [Black Gold](/guides/black-gold-durian-guide) or [old tree](/guides/old-tree-musang-king) often function as grades above the top grade.

## Why grades don't transfer between stalls

Stall A's Grade A and Stall B's Grade A are two independent judgments made by two people looking at two different lots. Neither is wrong, and neither is comparable.

A stall that sources well and grades strictly may sell Grade B fruit that beats another stall's Grade A. A stall that sources poorly may have nothing worth the top label on a given day and use it anyway.

Grades also move within the same stall over the season. Early or late in a harvest, or after bad weather, the whole lot can be weaker. The seller's "A" that week may just mean "the best of what came in," not "as good as last month's A."

This is also why comparing prices across stalls by grade alone is misleading. Our [guide to why durian prices change](/guides/why-durian-prices-change) goes into how grade and freshness create price gaps that look like bargains or rip-offs but aren't either.

## How to check the grade yourself

You can't audit a grading system, but you can inspect a durian. These checks work regardless of what the sign says.

**1. Ask them to open it before you pay.** A reputable stall will. If a seller charging a premium grade won't let you see the flesh first, don't buy it.

**2. Count and press the pods.** Look for full chambers and plump pods. Gently press one — it should feel dense, not soggy.

**3. Look at the colour in daylight or good light.** Stall lighting can flatter pale flesh. Deep, even gold is what the top grade should show.

**4. Check the surface.** Fresh pods have a smooth, slightly glossy skin. A dry film, cracks, or browning mean the fruit has been open or dropped too long, and that isn't premium at any grade.

**5. Taste a pod.** If you're paying for the top tier, it should taste noticeably richer than the regular tier at the same stall. If you honestly can't tell them apart, buy the cheaper one.

**6. Ask what came in today.** Freshness often matters more than grade. A same-day regular fruit can easily beat a two-day-old premium one.

## When the premium grade is worth paying for

Grade premiums aren't a scam by default. Sorting fruit takes skill, and the best fruit in any lot is genuinely scarce. The question is whether *this* fruit earns *this* premium.

**Pay for the top grade if** you're buying a small amount for a special occasion, you know and like the dense, bitter end of Musang King, and the stall opens the fruit so you can see what you're getting.

**Buy regular if** you're feeding a group, you're new to Musang King, or you're mostly after the flavour rather than the perfect pod. Fresh regular-grade Musang King is what most people are actually happy eating.

**Buy budget grade if** you're making desserts or freezing pulp, where appearance doesn't matter and a few thin pods cost you nothing.

**Walk away if** the premium gap is large and the fruit in front of you doesn't look different from the regular tier.

## The verdict

Musang King grades are a stall's shorthand for "better" and "worse" versions of the same durian. They're useful inside one stall, especially one you trust, and close to meaningless when comparing across stalls.

Treat the grade as the seller's opening claim. Then look at the pods, press them, taste one, and ask when the fruit came in. If the fruit matches the grade, pay for it happily. If it doesn't, the label doesn't make it better.
