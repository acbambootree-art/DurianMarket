Golden Phoenix is the durian that splits a table. Half the group will say it's too pungent, too bitter, too wet. The other half will quietly finish the box. If you eat durian for the bitterness rather than the sweetness, this is a variety worth knowing — and it usually costs a good deal less than the fruit everyone else is queueing for.

Here's what **Golden Phoenix (Jin Feng, 金凤, D198)** actually is, and how to buy a good one in Singapore.

## What is Golden Phoenix?

Golden Phoenix is a Malaysian cultivar registered as **D198**, most strongly associated with **Johor**. That matters for Singapore buyers: Johor fruit has the shortest trip to our stalls, so Golden Phoenix tends to arrive fresh and in reasonable volume during the main seasons.

The fruit itself is usually **small** — often noticeably smaller than a Musang King or a Black Thorn on the same table. Don't read that as a defect. The seeds inside are small too, and the flesh-to-seed ratio is frequently better than the size of the husk suggests.

## What it tastes like

Golden Phoenix is not a beginner's durian, and it doesn't pretend to be:

- **Flavour:** properly bitter, with a sharp, almost alcoholic finish. Sweetness is present but sits behind the bitterness rather than leading.
- **Texture:** wet and soft — closer to a thick cream than a dense custard. It doesn't hold its shape the way Musang King does, and that's the variety behaving normally, not a sign of over-ripeness.
- **Colour:** pale. Whitish-yellow to light cream, much lighter than the golden-yellow of Musang King or the orange cast of [Red Prawn](/guides/red-prawn-durian-guide).
- **Aroma:** strong. This is one of the more pungent varieties on a Singapore stall. If you're eating at home in a flat with neighbours who complain, factor that in.
- **Seeds:** small, often flat. Good news for how much you actually eat per kilogram.

The pale flesh is the giveaway. Once opened, Golden Phoenix looks unlike almost anything else on the table, which makes it one of the harder varieties to fake.

## Why it's underrated

Three things work in Golden Phoenix's favour:

1. **It's cheap for what it delivers.** Bitterness is what people pay premiums for, and Golden Phoenix supplies it without a premium name attached.
2. **Small seeds.** A small fruit with flat seeds can out-yield a bigger fruit with fat ones. Judge by flesh, not husk.
3. **Johor proximity.** Shorter supply chain means fresher fruit more often during the main harvest windows.

The catch is that its texture — genuinely wet — reads as "spoiled" to buyers who only know the dense-custard style. It isn't. But it does mean the window between perfect and over-ripe is narrower than with firmer varieties.

## How to pick a good one

Because wet is normal here, the usual "is it too soft?" test misleads you. Use these instead:

- **Ask for it opened before you pay.** Standard practice at any stall worth buying from. Look at the colour first: pale cream is right, deep gold means you're being sold something else.
- **Smell past the pungency.** Golden Phoenix is meant to be loud. What you're checking for is a **sour or fermented edge** on top of that — that's the over-ripe signal, not the strength of the smell itself.
- **Check the seeds.** Lift a piece. Small flat seeds are the variety's advantage; a fat seed under a thin coat means you're paying for weight you can't eat.
- **Look for pooling.** Wet is fine. Liquid sitting in the chamber is not — that fruit has gone past its window.
- **Buy small deliberately.** Don't let a seller upsell you to a bigger fruit "for value". Size isn't where the value is in this variety.

Golden Phoenix isn't a common mislabeling target — there's no money in passing something off as a cheaper durian. The realistic risk is the reverse: pale, wet fruit being talked up as something premium. If a stall is calling pale cream-coloured flesh Musang King, walk. ## When to buy it

Golden Phoenix follows the main Malaysian durian seasons, with the strongest supply during the mid-year peak and a smaller later wave. In peak weeks, Johor fruit is plentiful and this is one of the best-value bitter durians you can put on a table.

Off-season, the calculation changes. Thin supply means the fruit on display has often travelled further or sat longer, and a wet-fleshed variety punishes that more than a firm one does. If it's deep off-season and the flesh looks tired, buy something more forgiving instead.

A practical habit: check the [tracker](/) before you head out and note the spread between Golden Phoenix and the premium tier. When that gap is wide, this is the smart buy of the evening.

## Who should buy Golden Phoenix

**Buy it if:**

- Bitterness is the reason you eat durian
- You want a strong, pungent fruit without paying premium prices
- You don't mind — or actively like — a wet, creamy texture
- You're buying for durian regulars rather than a mixed group

**Skip it if:**

- You're introducing someone to durian for the first time
- You want the dense, fudgy texture of Musang King or Black Thorn
- Strong smell is a problem where you're eating it
- It's deep off-season and supply looks thin

## The bottom line

Golden Phoenix is a bitter, pungent, wet-fleshed durian that costs less than it should because it doesn't look or feel like the varieties Singapore has been trained to want. If you already know you like bitter, it's one of the best-value fruits on the stall. If you don't, it will be the worst durian you've ever eaten — and that's a fair warning, not a criticism of the fruit.
