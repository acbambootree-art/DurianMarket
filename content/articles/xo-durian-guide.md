XO is the durian people either order every single time or refuse to touch. There is very little middle ground, and that's not an accident — it is the most polarising thing on most Singapore stall boards, and it's polarising for a specific, understandable reason: it tastes like it has been drinking.

If you have ever been handed a seed by an uncle who said "try this one, very shiok" and then watched him grin while you chewed, it was probably XO. Here's what it actually is, and how to buy one that's bitter on purpose rather than bitter by accident.

## What is XO durian?

XO is named after XO cognac, and the name is doing exactly the work you think it is. The flesh carries a fermented, alcoholic edge — a boozy sharpness on top of a deep bitterness — and the trade named it after the drink it reminds people of.

It is generally understood in the trade to be a D24 lineage durian that has been allowed to develop further on the tree, with the fermentation character coming from that extra time rather than from anything done to the fruit after it drops. Stalls treat it as its own named variety, price it as its own thing, and put it on the board as "XO" rather than as a number — so for buying purposes, treat it as its own variety regardless of where it came from.

On most boards it sits in the upper-mid band: above the everyday sweet varieties, below the premium tier that [Musang King and Black Thorn](/guides/black-thorn-vs-musang-king) occupy. ## What it tastes like

XO is the closest thing durian has to an acquired-taste category all its own:

- **Flavour:** heavily bitter with an alcoholic, fermented finish. Sweetness is present but sits underneath — it is not the point. The aftertaste lingers longer than most varieties.
- **Colour:** pale, whitish-yellow. It is one of the palest things on the table, and noticeably lighter than [D24](/guides/musang-king-vs-d24) despite the family connection.
- **Texture:** wet and soft, sometimes properly runny. XO is not a fruit you eat elegantly.
- **Aroma:** strong and sharp, with the same boozy note the flavour has. This is the one that announces itself in a lift.
- **Seeds:** variable. Judge the fruit in front of you rather than the reputation.

The honest summary: XO is what you buy when you have stopped eating durian for sweetness and started eating it for intensity. First-timers rarely enjoy it, and there's no shame in that — it isn't a beginner's fruit and nobody who sells it pretends otherwise.

## XO vs the varieties you already know

| | XO | D24 | Tekka | Musang King (D197) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flesh colour | Pale, whitish-yellow | Pale yellow | Pale yellow | Golden yellow |
| Flavour | Strongly bitter, alcoholic | Balanced bittersweet | Sweet, mild bitter finish | Intense bittersweet |
| Texture | Wet, soft, sometimes runny | Creamy, firmer | Soft, wet, creamy | Dense, fudgy |
| Aroma | Strong, sharp, boozy | Moderate–strong | Moderate–strong | Strong |
| Price tier | Upper-mid | Mid–upper-mid | Mid | Premium |
| Who it's for | Bitter chasers | Most people | Value hunters | Everyone with the budget |

The comparison that matters most is XO against D24, because the two get confused at the stall and because the price gap between them is usually small enough to make it a live decision. D24 is the balanced one — bitter and sweet in proportion, creamier, firmer, safer to put in front of a mixed table. XO takes the bitterness, pushes it much further, adds the alcoholic note, and loosens the texture. If D24 already tastes too bitter to you, XO is not a step you want to take.

If you're on the other side — D24 tastes flat, [Tekka](/guides/tekka-durian-guide) tastes like a hint of something you want more of — XO is one of the cheaper ways to get real intensity without paying the premium tier's asking price. Its closest rival for that job is [Golden Phoenix](/guides/golden-phoenix-durian-guide), which is bitter without the alcoholic note — worth trying both before you decide which kind of bitter you actually like.

## Why some people hate it

Worth stating plainly, because it saves money: a lot of people find XO genuinely unpleasant, and they're not wrong to.

The alcoholic note that fans describe as complexity reads to other people as spoilage. That's a reasonable confusion, because it's the same family of flavour. Fermentation on purpose and fermentation by accident taste more alike than anyone selling durian will admit. Which is exactly why buying XO badly is worse than buying most other varieties badly — you lose your ability to tell.

The practical consequence is that XO is the wrong durian to buy from a stall you don't trust, and the wrong durian to buy unopened.

## How to pick a good one

- **Always have it opened before you pay.** Non-negotiable for XO. Pale, whitish flesh is your variety confirmation, and it's the only check you can do in three seconds.
- **Boozy is fine. Sour is not.** This is the whole skill with XO. A deep, warm, fermented note that sits alongside the bitterness is correct. A thin, sharp, vinegary sourness is a fruit that has gone off. When in doubt, walk.
- **Wet, not weeping.** XO runs soft by nature, but the flesh should still hold shape when lifted. Flesh that has collapsed into liquid at the bottom of the husk has gone past.
- **Look at the flesh edges.** Browning, or a dried skin forming over the surface, means the fruit has been open too long in the heat.
- **Buy it where they cut a lot of it.** Turnover matters more for XO than for the firm varieties, because a slow table gives a wet durian time to become an actually spoiled one.

Counterfeiting isn't the main risk here the way it is at the premium end — the more common problem is the reverse: unremarkable pale-fleshed fruit sold as XO because the name gives a stall permission to charge more. Pale flesh alone isn't XO. The bitterness and the alcoholic finish are. Taste before you accept the price, and compare what stalls near you are asking in the [area directory](/directory).

## When to buy it

XO follows the main Malaysian harvests like everything else, and it's worth buying deep in season rather than at the edges. A wet, soft variety that depends on being caught at exactly the right moment does not do well in a thin week when stock has been sitting.

Its price also behaves differently from the premium tier. When Musang King spikes on demand, the upper-mid band tends to move less — so peak-hype weeks are when the value argument for XO is strongest. That gap is visible on the [trends page](/trends), and it's the single most useful thing to check before you commit to the front row of the display.

## Who should buy XO

**Buy it if:**

- You eat durian for bitterness, not sweetness
- D24 has started tasting too tame
- You like the fermented, boozy end of things generally
- You want premium-tier intensity without premium-tier pricing
- You're buying from a stall you trust and can taste before paying

**Skip it if:**

- You're new to durian, or buying for someone who is
- You want creamy and sweet
- Bitterness is something you tolerate rather than chase
- You're buying unopened, off-season, or from a table you don't know
- You're feeding a mixed group — one XO will split the table

## The bottom line

XO is the bitter, alcoholic, deliberately fermented end of the durian spectrum: pale flesh, wet texture, a finish that tastes like a drink. It is excellent if that's what you want and genuinely unpleasant if it isn't, and there's no version of it that splits the difference.

Buy it opened, buy it in season, buy it somewhere that moves stock, and learn the difference between boozy and sour before you spend real money on it. Check today's spread on the [price tracker](/) and find a stall near you in the [directory](/directory).
