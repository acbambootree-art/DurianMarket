Watch an uncle work a durian pile and it looks like superstition. He picks one up, turns it, taps it, sniffs the base, shakes it near his ear, puts it down, picks up the next. Twenty seconds a fruit, no hesitation.

None of it is mystical. Every one of those moves is testing a specific thing — ripeness, dryness, whether the flesh has separated from the shell, whether the fruit has been sitting too long. You can learn the same checks in an afternoon. What you can't shortcut is the thousands of fruits he has opened afterwards to find out whether he was right.

Here's what each check is actually for, ranked by how much it's worth to a buyer.

## The one rule that beats every other trick

**Ask for it opened before you pay.**

Everything below this line is a way of guessing what's inside a closed shell. Opening it is not a guess. Most Singapore stalls will open a durian in front of you and let you look before money changes hands — that is the norm, not a favour, and a stall that refuses is telling you something.

The rest of this guide matters for two situations: choosing which fruit to ask them to open, and buying whole uncut durian to take home. If you're buying opened, at a stall, in person, skip ahead to judging the flesh.

## The stem tells you when it fell

Durian is not picked — good durian drops when it's ready. The stem is the clock on that drop.

- **Fresh and slightly moist at the cut** means it came off the tree recently.
- **Dry, shrivelled, or dark and woody** means it's been sitting. Days, not hours.
- **Green under a fingernail scratch** is a fresh stem. Brown all the way through is old.
- **A thick, sturdy stem** is generally a sign of a well-fed fruit. A thin, weedy one often carries less flesh.

The trick that cuts the other way: stems get trimmed. A stall can cut back a dried stem to expose fresher-looking wood underneath. If a stem looks suspiciously short and freshly sliced across a wide, woody base, you're looking at a haircut, not a fresh drop.

## The shake tells you if it's ready

Hold the durian close to your ear and give it a firm, controlled shake — not a rattle, a small movement.

What you want is a slight, soft shift inside. That's flesh that has ripened enough to come loose from the shell wall. It means the fruit is ready.

What you don't want:

- **Nothing at all.** Flesh still gripping the shell — likely underripe, likely firmer and less aromatic than the variety should be.
- **A hard knock.** A loose, solid thud is often a dried-out seed rattling in a shrunken pod. The fruit is past it.

This is the check that separates people who buy uncut durian from people who shouldn't. It takes real repetition to calibrate, because the right answer varies by variety — a dense, fudgy [Musang King](/guides/musang-king-vs-d24) moves differently from a soft, wet [Tekka](/guides/tekka-durian-guide).

## The smell test is done at the bottom

Sellers sniff the base of the fruit, where the shell segments meet, not the sides. That's where aroma escapes first as the fruit opens up internally.

- **Strong, sweet, unmistakably durian** — good, and ready to eat now.
- **Faint or almost nothing** — underripe, or a variety with a mild aroma. Ask which.
- **Sour, boozy, or alcoholic** — fermentation. This is spoilage, not "extra bitter." Walk away.
- **A whiff of ammonia** — over-ripe. Same answer.

The distinction that costs people money: bitterness and fermentation are not the same thing. A properly bitter durian smells intense but clean. A fermented one smells sharp and slightly wrong, like it's turning into something else — because it is.

## Weight, shape, and the seams

Pick up two durians of the same size. The heavier one usually has more flesh; the lighter one has more air, more shell, or dried-out pods. Sellers do this constantly and it's the easiest check to copy — you're comparing, not measuring.

Shape matters because durian ripens unevenly. A fruit with plump, well-defined lobes tends to have full pods all round. A lopsided one often has one good chamber and two thin ones — and you pay by the kilogram of whole fruit either way.

The seams — the lines running from stem to base where the shell will split — should be visible and slightly raised. Some sellers press along a seam to feel whether it gives. A fruit that has already cracked open is not automatically bad, but it's on a clock: exposed flesh dries, browns, and picks up whatever is in the air.

## Judging the flesh once it's open

This is where the real decision happens, and where most of your money is protected.

| What you see | What it means |
|---|---|
| Even colour, matching the variety | Fruit is what the sign says |
| A dry, papery skin on the surface | Cut too long ago |
| Browning at the edges | Sitting out, likely in heat |
| Flesh pooling or slumping | Past ripe, gone watery |
| Seeds much larger than expected | Less flesh per kilogram than you're paying for |
| Grey or dull patches | Reject it |

Colour is also your variety check. Golden yellow, bright orange, pale yellow and orange-pink are different varieties, not different moods of the same fruit — the [full variety comparison](/guides/durian-varieties-singapore) lays out which is which. If the flesh doesn't match the name on the card, that's the conversation to have before you pay, not after.

Taste one pod at the stall if they offer. Bitterness, sweetness and texture are variety traits; sourness is not. Sour means send it back.

## What sellers know that you can't check

Two things worth being honest about.

**Provenance.** Where the fruit came from, which farm, how old the trees are, when it landed in Singapore — you cannot see any of this in the shell. You're trusting the stall. That's the actual product a good durian seller sells, and it's why regulars keep going back to the same table rather than shopping on price.

**The pile.** Sellers know which crate arrived this morning and which has been worked over for two days. You're picking from what's in front of you; they know what it is. Buying early in the day, from a stall that's visibly moving stock, does more for your odds than any tapping technique.

## A practical routine

1. Ask what varieties are on and what came in today.
2. Compare weight between two similar-sized fruits.
3. Check the stem — fresh, thick, not obviously trimmed.
4. Smell the base. Sweet and strong, not sour.
5. Shake gently. A soft shift, not a knock.
6. Ask them to open it before you pay.
7. Look at colour, edges, and seeds. Taste a pod if offered.
8. If it's wrong, say so at the stall. After you've walked away, it's yours.

Know the going rate before you start — it's much easier to reject a bad fruit when you know what a fair number looks like. ## The bottom line

The stem, the shake and the smell are real checks, worth learning, and worth maybe sixty percent of what an experienced seller knows. The other forty percent is knowing the stall and the stock — which you get by going back to the same one until they recognise you.

Until then: buy in season, buy from a stall that's turning over fruit, and never pay for a durian you haven't seen opened.
