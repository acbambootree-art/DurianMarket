You've bought a good one. Now you have to get it home. This is the part where people improvise — a plastic bag, a hopeful face, a train ride — and occasionally get stopped at the gantry.

The short version: MRT and public buses are a firm no. Grab and taxis are a maybe, entirely up to the driver. Planes are a no for fresh durian on essentially every airline. Here's the detail behind each, and what to do instead.

## The MRT: banned, and it's the famous one

Durians are prohibited on Singapore's MRT and on public buses. You'll have seen the sign inside the train carriage — the row of pictograms for no smoking, no eating or drinking, no flammable goods, and a durian at the end.

The durian is the one pictogram with no fine printed next to it. That quirk has become a national in-joke, and it's the source of a persistent myth that the ban is "not really enforced" or "not really a rule." It is a rule. Staff can and do stop people at the gantry and ask them to leave with the fruit. What's true is that there is no published dollar penalty, so in practice enforcement means being turned away rather than being fined.

A few things worth knowing:

- **The ban covers whole fruit and open durian.** A sealed box of frozen pulp or a durian puff from a bakery is a different thing entirely — that's packaged food, not a durian, and nobody is stopping you.
- **Sealing it well does not make it legal.** Even a triple-bagged, boxed durian is still a durian. Some people get through without comment; that's discretion, not permission.
- **Buses follow the same rule.** Same operators, same prohibition, and a bus is smaller and hotter than a train carriage.

If you're on public transport regularly and durian season is on, this is the single biggest argument for either eating at the stall or getting it delivered.

## Grab, taxis, and private hire: ask first

There is no law banning durian from a taxi or a private hire car. There is, however, a driver — and the car is their livelihood. If the smell settles into the upholstery, they lose fares.

So the rule here is social rather than legal: **drivers can refuse, and many will.** Some will cancel the moment they see you standing there with a bag. That's their right, and it isn't personal.

How to do it properly:

1. **Say so upfront.** Message the driver through the app before they arrive: you have durian, it's boxed and sealed, is that okay. A driver who agrees in advance won't cancel on arrival, and one who says no has cost you two minutes instead of a ride.
2. **Have it boxed, not bagged.** A sealed box inside a bag is a completely different proposition to a knotted plastic bag. Ask the stall — most will box on request, and many will do it without being asked if you say you're taking a Grab.
3. **Put it in the boot.** Offer, don't assume. It keeps the smell out of the cabin where the next passenger sits.
4. **Ask for fresh air, not recirculate.** Recirculating pushes the aroma through the vents and into the cabin filter, which is how a fifteen-minute ride becomes a month-long problem for the driver.
5. **Don't argue if refused.** Cancel, rebook, mention it in the next chat. The sooner you say it, the easier it goes.

If the smell does get into a car — yours or anyone's — our guide to [getting rid of durian smell](/guides/remove-durian-smell) covers what actually removes it versus what just masks it.

## Planes: assume no, check anyway

Fresh durian is prohibited on essentially every commercial airline, in the cabin and in checked baggage. It isn't classed as dangerous cargo; it's an odour and passenger-comfort rule, and airlines enforce it as a condition of carriage. Singapore Airlines and the region's major carriers all prohibit it.

Two practical points:

- **Processed and vacuum-sealed durian products are a separate question.** Frozen pulp in commercial vacuum packaging, durian snacks, mooncakes and pastries are often fine — but this is airline-specific and destination-specific, and the packaging has to be genuinely sealed. Check the airline's conditions of carriage before you pack, not at the counter.
- **The destination country's rules matter too.** Fresh fruit is restricted or prohibited on arrival in many countries regardless of what the airline allows. Australia, New Zealand and the US have strict agricultural controls. Getting it onto the plane and getting it through customs are two different hurdles.

If you're taking durian overseas as a gift, buy a commercially packed frozen or freeze-dried product designed for travel. It's the only version of this that reliably works.

## What to do instead

Three options cover almost every situation.

**Eat it at the stall.** This is what the stall is set up for — a table, gloves, tissue, a bin for the husks, and someone to open it properly. You get the fruit at its best, you carry nothing home, and your kitchen stays clean. Use the [directory](/directory) to find a stall near where you already are.

**Get it delivered.** Delivery exists precisely because transporting durian is annoying. It arrives packed, and the smell problem belongs to someone whose vehicle is built for it. Our guide on [choosing a durian delivery service](/guides/best-durian-delivery-singapore) covers what to look for, and [stall versus delivery](/guides/stall-vs-delivery) weighs the trade-offs on price and freshness.

**Drive, and pack it properly.** If you have a car, this is straightforward: sealed box, in the boot, straight home, don't leave it parked in the sun. Then deal with it the same evening — leftover flesh into an airtight container per our [storage guide](/guides/how-to-store-durian), or portioned and frozen if you've overbought.

## One more thing about the husks

The transport problem doesn't end when you get home. Husks in an indoor bin overnight are the single most common reason a flat smells of durian the next morning, and in a HDB block that becomes a corridor-wide issue.

Bag them and take them down the same night. If you ate at the stall, this problem is already solved — which is, quietly, the strongest argument for eating there.
