Singapore does not grow its durian. Almost everything on a stall table here came across the Causeway from Malaysia — mostly Johor and Pahang, with fruit from further north arriving at different points in the year. So "durian season in Singapore" really means "durian season in the orchards that supply Singapore," and that is a moving target.

The short answer most people want: there is a big season around the middle of the year and a smaller one towards the end of it. The useful answer is that neither has fixed dates, and the difference between a good week and a bad week is bigger than the difference between one stall and another.

## The two seasons

**The main season** runs through the middle of the year. This is the one people mean when they say "durian season" — the weeks when Geylang gets loud, prices soften, and the good varieties are actually available rather than theoretical. Volume is high, choice is wide, and this is when Musang King, D24, Black Thorn and the rest are all on the table at the same time.

**The secondary season** comes later in the year and is smaller, shorter, and less reliable. Some years it is a genuine second harvest with decent fruit at decent rates. Other years it barely shows up. Treat it as a bonus rather than something to plan around.

Between and around those, there is off-season fruit. It exists, it is real durian, and it is expensive — thin supply, fewer good lots, and stalls that need the margin to justify opening. The variety selection narrows too. Off-season is when you are most likely to be offered something that isn't quite what the sign says, which is why our [guide to spotting fake Musang King](/guides/how-to-spot-fake-musang-king) gets its heaviest use in the quiet months.

## Why the dates move every year

The harvest follows the trees, and the trees follow the weather. A few months before any fruit reaches a stall, the orchards flower. What happens in that window sets everything downstream.

**Rain at the wrong time.** Heavy rain during flowering knocks blossoms off and thins the eventual crop. Rain during the drop damages fruit on the ground and reduces the proportion that is worth selling.

**Dry spells.** A dry stretch before flowering tends to encourage a heavier flowering, which is part of why some years produce a wave and others produce a trickle.

**Region.** Johor, Pahang and the northern states do not peak at the same time. This is why the season feels staggered in Singapore — one region's fruit tapers as another's arrives, and the varieties on the table shift with it.

**The trees themselves.** Durian trees do not produce evenly year on year. A heavy year is often followed by a lighter one. Nobody can plan around this, including the farmers.

The practical takeaway: last year's peak week is a weak predictor of this year's. Anyone quoting you exact season dates months in advance is guessing.

## What each phase feels like at the stall

| Phase | Supply | Prices | What's on the table |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early season | Rising, patchy | Still high, falling | First lots, uneven quality |
| Peak | Heavy | Lowest of the year | Full variety range, best picking |
| Late season | Tapering | Climbing again | Fewer varieties, more clearance |
| Off-season | Thin | Highest | Limited choice, more frozen stock |

Early season is the trap. Supply looks like it has arrived, prices have started to move, and the fruit is genuinely available — but the first lots off the trees are often not the best lots. If you can wait two or three weeks into a season rather than buying the first fruit you see, you generally eat better for less.

## How to tell where you are in the season

You do not need a calendar for this. You need to read the market, and the market tells you plainly.

**Price direction beats price level.** A rate that has been falling for two weeks means supply is building. A rate climbing means the wave is passing. Our [price trends page](/trends) shows the direction; the [live tracker](/) shows where things stand today. Direction is the more useful of the two if you have any flexibility about when you buy.

**Variety count.** Count how many named varieties a decent stall is carrying. Peak season stalls carry many; off-season stalls carry two or three and a lot of qualifiers. If you want to know what a full table should look like, the [variety comparison guide](/guides/durian-varieties-singapore) covers what is normally in circulation here.

**How hard they are selling.** In peak season, stalls do not need to talk you into anything — the fruit moves. Heavy persuasion, unusual "special deals," and a lot of explaining about why this particular fruit is worth the price are all signals that stock is thin.

**Whether opened fruit is offered freely.** Stalls with volume will happily open one for you to look at. Stalls with scarce stock get protective. That single behaviour tells you more about supply than any price does.

## Timing your buying

If you eat durian a few times a year and want to eat it well:

1. **Do not chase the first fruit of the season.** Let the wave build. The gap between week one and week three is usually significant in both price and quality.
2. **Buy in the middle, buy more.** Peak weeks are when to do a big group buy, a durian party, or your annual splurge on the expensive varieties.
3. **Check direction before you go.** Two minutes on [trends](/trends) tells you whether waiting a few days is likely to help.
4. **Use the [area directory](/directory)** to see what different parts of Singapore are asking on the same day — the spread between areas widens when supply is tight.
5. **In off-season, adjust expectations rather than budget.** You will pay more for less choice. That is the season, not the stall cheating you. If the goal is simply to eat durian rather than to eat a specific variety, this is when flexibility saves you the most money.

## A note on "season" in a delivery world

Delivery services and frozen pulp have blurred the edges. You can get durian in Singapore essentially year-round now, and that is genuinely useful. But availability is not the same as season — the fruit available in a thin month is thinner fruit at a thicker price, whether it comes from a stall or a van.

Frozen pulp is the honest exception. It is harvested in season, frozen at peak, and sold later. It is not the same eating experience as fresh fruit an hour off the truck, but it is not a compromise on the fruit itself either.

## The short version

Two seasons, one big and one small, both moving by weeks year to year depending on rain and region. Do not memorise dates. Watch price direction on [trends](/trends), check the [current rate](/) before you leave the house, and let a season build for a couple of weeks before you buy seriously. Then use the ordinary checks from our [guide to picking a good durian](/guides/how-to-choose-a-durian) — because even in the best week of the best season, the fruit in front of you still has to be a good one.
